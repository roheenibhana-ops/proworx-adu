ADU Permits in Utah: What Every Homeowner Should Know Before Building

Pro-Worx ADU Team · Utah ADU Guides · Updated September 2026

What Permits Does an ADU Need in Utah?

Every ADU in Utah needs at least two approvals: a zoning or land-use permit confirming the unit is allowed on your lot, and a building permit covering the actual construction. If you plan to rent the unit out, most cities also require a separate rental dwelling or business license after construction is finished. Timelines vary by city and by how complete your application is on the first submission, but the process below is the same shape everywhere in Utah.

What Permits and Approvals Do You Actually Need?

Most Utah homeowners picture "getting a permit" as a single form. In practice, an ADU usually touches four separate approvals, and missing one is a common reason projects stall partway through:

ApprovalWhat It CoversWhen You Need It
Zoning / land-use permitConfirms the ADU is allowed on your specific lot under your city's zoning codeBefore building permit review starts
Building permitCovers the actual construction: structural, electrical, plumbing, mechanicalBefore construction begins
Utility connection approvalConfirms your water provider and sewer or septic system can support a second unitDuring plan review, before final sign-off
Rental dwelling or business licenseLegal authorization to lease the finished unit to a tenantAfter construction, before renting

Source: process compiled from Utah city ADU permitting guides, including Draper City's ADU permit page.

The zoning and building permits are non-negotiable everywhere. The rental license only matters if you intend to lease the unit, but skipping it doesn't just risk a fine, it can also mean a tenant's lease isn't actually enforceable if a dispute ever ends up in court. If you're building specifically to rent, it's worth confirming your city's licensing requirement at the same time you confirm your zoning, not after the unit is finished.

What Does the Process Look Like Step by Step?

The exact order and paperwork differ slightly by city, but the shape of the process is consistent across Utah:

  • 1. Confirm jurisdiction and zone. City or county, and which zoning district your lot sits in, since ADU rules can differ block to block near a zoning boundary.
  • 2. Identify your ADU type. Internal, garage conversion, basement conversion, or detached, since each has different size limits, setback rules and sometimes different application forms.
  • 3. Verify physical feasibility. Egress, utility capacity, and parking, ideally before you fall in love with a floor plan that your lot can't actually support.
  • 4. Prepare your application package. Site plan, floor plans, elevations, and any required owner-occupancy affidavit.
  • 5. Submit for zoning and building review. Most cities review both together or in close sequence.
  • 6. Respond to any correction letters. Zoning, utility, or fire-safety questions are the most common.
  • 7. Build, then pass inspections. Framing, electrical, plumbing, and a final inspection before occupancy.
  • 8. Apply for a rental license, if you plan to lease the unit.

Source: process steps compiled from Utah ADU permitting guides and city planning department requirements.

Step 4 is where most delays actually get created, weeks before anyone notices, because an incomplete site plan or a missing utility letter doesn't cause a problem immediately. It causes a problem when the reviewer gets to it, sends a correction letter, and your application goes back in the queue behind everyone who submitted after you but submitted complete. Getting it right the first time is almost always faster than getting it in early but incomplete.

What Building Code Requirements Catch People Off Guard?

Utah has adopted the International Residential Code (IRC), and a few of its provisions are the most common reason a basement or garage conversion needs more work than a homeowner initially expects:

  • Egress windows. Any basement habitable room, bedrooms especially, needs an emergency escape opening with at least 5.7 square feet of net clear area, a minimum 20-inch width and 24-inch height, and a sill no higher than 44 inches above the finished floor. On a basement with small existing windows, this often means cutting a larger opening and adding a window well, real excavation work, not just a fixture swap.
  • Ceiling height. Habitable basement rooms generally need adequate ceiling height under the IRC, and a basement with ductwork, beams or a low slab-to-joist measurement can fall short, sometimes requiring the floor to be dropped or the mechanical layout reworked.
  • Separate systems. A true ADU needs its own kitchen and bathroom, which usually means new plumbing runs, not just tapping into the main home's existing lines, and often a dedicated electrical sub-panel depending on the unit's total load.
  • Fire separation. Attached and internal ADUs typically need fire-rated separation between the unit and the primary home, an assembly requirement that has to be built to spec, not added after the fact.

Source: International Residential Code egress requirements as summarized by egresswindows.com; ceiling height and fire separation are general IRC provisions, and exact figures should be confirmed with your city's adopted code edition.

None of these are reasons to avoid a basement conversion, they're genuinely one of the most cost-effective ADU paths, as we cover in our cost guide. They're reasons to have someone check your existing basement against code before you commit to a floor plan, since an egress or ceiling-height problem found during framing costs a lot more to fix than one found during the initial walkthrough.

How Does SB284 Change Permitting on October 1, 2026?

Utah's SB284 sets a new statewide floor for detached ADUs starting October 1, 2026, but it doesn't remove local permitting, it changes what cities are allowed to require. Cities still control their own setbacks, height limits and owner-occupancy rules, and every property still needs to go through zoning and building review. What changes is that some properties that were previously ineligible for a detached ADU, because of an older, more restrictive city ordinance, become eligible once cities update their codes to meet the new state minimum. We cover the specifics, including real examples from Lehi's updated ordinance, in our full SB284 breakdown.

A detached ADU in Lehi

A Lehi homeowner we spoke with had been told a few years ago that their lot was too small for a detached ADU under the city's older ordinance. What Actually Causes Delays or Rejections?

In our experience, the same handful of issues account for most permitting slowdowns:

CategoryCommon Issue
DocumentationMissing or incomplete site plans, floor plans, or owner-occupancy affidavits
Zoning/siteLot too small for the desired ADU type, setback conflicts, parking that can't be satisfied on site
UtilitiesSewer, water or electrical capacity that needs upgrading before approval
Code complianceMissing egress windows, insufficient ceiling height, fire-separation gaps
Prior workUnpermitted existing construction that surfaces during review
HOA/CC&RsPrivate restrictions separate from city zoning that still need to be checked

Source: common delay categories compiled from Utah ADU permitting guides and city planning requirements.

Parking is worth calling out specifically, since it's an easy thing to overlook: most cities that allow ADUs require at least one additional on-site parking space, and it generally can't be a tandem space or sit directly behind another vehicle. On a smaller lot, that single requirement can end up dictating where the entire unit gets placed.
